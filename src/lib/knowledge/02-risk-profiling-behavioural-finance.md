## RISK TOLERANCE FUNDAMENTALS

### Key Distinction
- **Clients** view risk as actual loss suffered (realized loss)
- **Financial institutions** view risk as volatility of return (standard deviation)
- **Myopic loss aversion**: Clients evaluate portfolios frequently and act on short-term gains/losses rather than focusing on long-term horizon (Benartzi & Thaler, 1993)

### Risk-Return Relationship
Risk and return are inextricably tied:
- If clients require a particular return level → they must accept accompanying risk
- If clients cannot tolerate beyond a certain risk level → they must accept the resulting return
- If calculations call for more aggressive strategy than client tolerates → client must either accept more risk, establish more modest goals, or spend less (save more)

### Factors Affecting Risk Tolerance

**Demographics:**
- Men generally accept higher portfolio risk than women
- Both genders become more risk-averse with age
- Both genders become more risk-tolerant with increased wealth

**Income Stability:**
- Stable income → more open to volatile securities
- Uncertain income/employment → prefer conservative, high-liquidity products (e.g., cashable GICs)
- If employment risk is independent of equity risk → can increase risky asset proportion

## RISK TOLERANCE QUESTIONNAIRE FRAMEWORK

### Standard Assessment Categories
1. Primary investment objective (aggressive growth → preservation of capital)
2. Investment time horizon (< 1 year → 10+ years)
3. Risk-return philosophy (returns most important → risk most important)
4. Return expectations (0–5% → 15%+)
5. Loss tolerance threshold (5–10% → never sell)
6. Benchmark importance (very important → not important)
7. Real return expectations vs. inflation + taxes
8. Income stability comfort level
9. Bond portfolio management preference (short → long term)
10. Equity portfolio management preference (maximize returns → income-focused)

### Limitations of Questionnaires
- Cannot provide full picture of risk tolerance
- Only provide guidelines for asset allocation
- Must be used in concert with behavioural assessment tools
- A pointed, purposeful conversation about risk is essential
- Clients may give contradictory answers (want high return AND no risk)

## BEHAVIOURAL FINANCE: INVESTOR BIASES

### Cognitive Biases (Faulty Reasoning — Easier to Correct)
Stem from errors in statistical processing, memory, or information processing. Can be corrected through education and better information.

| Bias | Definition | Impact on Investing |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Overconfidence** | Unwarranted faith in own reasoning and judgment | Overestimate ability to pick stocks; underestimate risks; trade too frequently |
| **Anchoring** | Getting "anchored" to initial information (e.g., purchase price) | Won't sell below purchase price regardless of fundamentals; biased future calculations |
| **Availability** | Estimating probability based on how easily examples come to mind | Choose funds based on advertising exposure, not merit; overweight recent dramatic events |
| **Confirmation** | Seeking information that confirms existing beliefs, ignoring contradicting evidence | Hold losing positions; miss warning signs; selectively filter research |
| **Conservatism** | Clinging to prior views despite new contradictory information | Slow to update portfolio despite changed fundamentals; underreact to new data |
| **Representativeness** | Judging probability of outcome by how much it resembles something familiar | Assume good company = good stock; extrapolate short-term trends indefinitely |
| **Recency** | Overweighting recent events over historical data | Chase recent performance; panic sell in downturns; ignore long-term fundamentals |
| **Framing** | Responding differently based on how question is presented | Different investment decisions based on whether framed as gain vs. loss avoidance |
| **Cognitive Dissonance** | Discomfort from holding conflicting beliefs | Rationalize poor investments to avoid admitting error; ignore contradictory evidence |
| **Self-Attribution** | Attributing successes to skill, failures to external factors | Overestimate investing ability; fail to learn from mistakes |
| **Illusion of Control** | Believing ability to control random outcomes | Excessive trading; overconcentration in familiar stocks |
| **Mental Accounting** | Treating money differently based on its source or intended use | Suboptimal allocation; fail to view portfolio holistically |

### Emotional Biases (Impulse-Driven — Harder to Correct)
Originate from feelings, impulses, or intuition rather than conscious calculation. More difficult to correct; often must be accommodated.

| Bias | Definition | Impact on Investing |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Loss Aversion** | Pain of losses felt roughly 2x more intensely than pleasure of equivalent gains | Hold losing investments too long; sell winners too quickly; avoid necessary risk |
| **Endowment** | Overvaluing assets already owned vs. equivalent assets not owned | Refuse to sell inherited securities; hold concentrated positions; anchored to existing portfolio |
| **Status Quo** | Preferring current state; choosing inaction over change | Fail to rebalance; maintain unsuitable allocations; avoid beneficial portfolio changes |
| **Regret Aversion** | Avoiding actions that might cause regret, even if beneficial | Hold losers to avoid realizing regret; follow herd behaviour; avoid unconventional strategies |
| **Self-Control** | Consuming today at the expense of saving for tomorrow | Insufficient retirement savings; prioritize immediate gratification; fail to follow financial plan |
| **Optimism** | Overestimating likelihood of positive outcomes | Underestimate risk; insufficient insurance; overly aggressive projections |

### Gender Tendencies
- **Men** more susceptible to: Overconfidence (C), Loss Aversion (E), Availability (C), Cognitive Dissonance (C)
- **Women** more susceptible to: Endowment (E), Status Quo (E), Representativeness (C), Regret Aversion (E)

## INVESTOR PERSONALITY TYPES

### Two Classification Dimensions

**Risk Tolerance Dimension:**
| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Idealist** | Willing to take risk. Higher return expectations. Can fall prey to speculative fads. Susceptible to: overconfidence, optimism, self-control bias |
| **Pragmatist** | Practical, realistic about risk-return tradeoffs. Typically NOT susceptible to idealist biases |

**Information Processing Dimension:**
| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Framer** | Processes information in parts. May compartmentalize investments (mental accounting). Susceptible to: anchoring, conservatism, mental accounting, framing, ambiguity aversion |
| **Integrator** | Processes information holistically. Broad view of financial picture. Typically NOT susceptible to framer biases |

### Four Personality Combinations
1. **Idealist + Framer** — Risk-tolerant but compartmentalizes. Watch for speculation within mental accounts.
2. **Idealist + Integrator** — Risk-tolerant with holistic view. Best positioned but watch for overconfidence.
3. **Pragmatist + Framer** — Conservative but compartmentalizes. May miss portfolio-level optimization.
4. **Pragmatist + Integrator** — Conservative with holistic view. Most balanced but may be too cautious.

## DIAGNOSING BIASES IN PRACTICE

### Diagnostic Approach for Loss Aversion
Present hypothetical scenarios:
- "If your portfolio dropped 20% in one month, what would you do?"
- "Would you rather have a guaranteed $5,000 or a 50% chance of $12,000?"
- "How did you feel when markets dropped 30% in 2020?" (captures emotional vs. theoretical risk tolerance)

### Bias → Allocation Impact

| Identified Bias | Allocation Adjustment |
|----------------|----------------------|
| Loss aversion | Consider slightly more conservative allocation than math suggests; emphasize downside protection |
| Overconfidence | Reduce concentrated positions; implement systematic rebalancing; limit active trading |
| Status quo | Automate rebalancing; present changes as adjustments rather than overhauls |
| Recency | Show long-term historical returns alongside recent; anchor to plan, not headlines |
| Mental accounting | Present unified portfolio view; consolidate strategy across accounts |
| Self-control | Automate savings; implement forced contribution schedules |

### Best Practical Allocation
The ideal portfolio balances quantitative optimization (efficient frontier) with behavioural reality. The "best practical allocation" is one the client can actually stick with through market ups and downs — even if it's not mathematically optimal.

> **MOSAIC APPLICATION:** The conversational risk assessment should combine standardized questions (like the 10-question framework) with open-ended probing questions that surface emotional biases. The AI should watch for contradictory responses (e.g., wanting aggressive growth + zero loss tolerance) and address them directly. The risk summary should be shown to the user for confirmation before plan generation.

## U.S. TAX OBLIGATIONS FOR CANADIAN RESIDENTS

### The Substantial Presence Test
Canadians may be considered U.S. residents for tax purposes if they meet the substantial presence test:

**Formula:**
```
Days in current year
+ (Days in prior year × 1/3)
+ (Days in year before that × 1/6)
= Weighted total
```

If weighted total ≥ 183 → potentially treated as U.S. tax resident.

**Exemptions:**
- **Closer Connection Exception**: If present in U.S. < 183 days in current year AND can demonstrate closer connection to Canada (tax home, family, personal belongings, bank accounts, driver's license, etc.) → file IRS Form 8840 annually
- **Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty (Article IV)**: treaty tie-breaker rules determine residence if both countries claim residency. Generally favours permanent home, centre of vital interests, habitual abode, citizenship.

### Implications of U.S. Tax Residency
- Must file U.S. tax returns on worldwide income
- Foreign tax credits available to avoid double taxation
- RRSP/RRIF income: protected under Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty (Article XVIII) — can elect to defer U.S. tax on RRSP/RRIF income
- TFSA: NOT recognized by IRS — income is taxable in the U.S.

## U.S. REAL PROPERTY

### Structuring the Purchase
| Structure | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|-----------|---------------|
| **Personal ownership** | Simple; capital gains exemption on sale of primary residence (limited) | U.S. estate tax exposure; probate in U.S. jurisdiction |
| **Canadian corporation** | May reduce estate tax; liability protection | FIRPTA withholding on sale; corporate tax complexity; potential double tax |
| **U.S. LLC** | Liability protection; flow-through taxation for U.S. purposes | Canada treats LLC as corporation (no flow-through); potential double taxation |
| **Cross-border trust** | Estate planning flexibility; may reduce estate tax | Complex; costly to establish and maintain |

**Recommendation:** Personal ownership is simplest for most snowbirds. Use Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty estate tax credits to manage estate exposure.

### U.S. Mortgage Considerations
| Feature | Canadian Mortgage | U.S. Mortgage |
|---------|------------------|---------------|
| Term | Typically 5-year term, 25-year amortization | 15 or 30-year fixed rate (fully amortized) |
| Prepayment | Penalties for prepayment within term | Generally no prepayment penalty |
| Interest deductibility | Not deductible on principal residence | Deductible on U.S. tax return (if filing) |
| Default | Recourse (lender can pursue borrower personally) | Non-recourse in some states |

### Taxation of U.S. Rental Income
- Must file U.S. tax return (Form 1040-NR)
- Two options:
  1. **Gross rental income** taxed at flat 30% withholding (no deductions) — simple but often more tax
  2. **Net rental income** election (file on time) — deduct expenses (property tax, insurance, maintenance, depreciation, mortgage interest) and pay tax at graduated rates — usually better
- Must also report on Canadian tax return; claim foreign tax credit for U.S. taxes paid

### Taxation on Sale of U.S. Real Property
- **FIRPTA** (Foreign Investment in Real Property Tax Act): buyer must withhold 15% of gross sale price and remit to IRS
- Can apply for reduced withholding if actual tax will be less
- Capital gain reported on both U.S. and Canadian returns
- Foreign tax credit on Canadian return for U.S. taxes paid
- Adjusted cost base: must account for U.S. depreciation claimed on rental property (recapture)

## U.S. INVESTMENT INCOME

### Dividends from U.S. Corporations
- Withholding tax: 15% under Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty (vs. 30% statutory rate)
- Report on Canadian return; claim foreign tax credit
- If held in RRSP: withholding exempt under Treaty
- If held in TFSA: 15% withholding applies (TFSA not recognized by IRS)

### Interest Income
- Generally exempt from U.S. withholding under Treaty (portfolio interest exemption)
- Must still report on Canadian return

### U.S. Capital Gains
- Non-resident Canadians generally NOT subject to U.S. tax on capital gains from U.S. securities (portfolio gains)
- Exception: U.S. real property (FIRPTA applies)

### Individual Taxpayer Identification Number (ITIN)
- Required for Canadians who need to file U.S. tax returns but don't have a Social Security Number
- Apply using IRS Form W-7
- Needed for: rental property owners, those claiming treaty benefits, those with U.S. source income requiring tax filing

## RETIREMENT INCOME — CROSS-BORDER

### Old Age Security (OAS)
- Portable — paid to Canadians living abroad if 20+ years of Canadian residence after age 18
- If < 20 years: payments stop after 6 months outside Canada
- OAS clawback may apply based on worldwide income
- Under Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty: OAS is taxable only in country of residence

### Canada/Quebec Pension Plan (CPP/QPP)
- Portable — paid regardless of country of residence
- Under Treaty: taxable only in country of residence
- CPP credit splitting on divorce applies regardless of where former spouse resides

### U.S. Social Security
- Canadians who worked in the U.S. may be eligible
- Canada-U.S. Social Security Totalization Agreement: can combine work periods in both countries to meet eligibility requirements
- Benefits taxable in country of residence under Treaty
- If receiving both CPP and U.S. Social Security: Windfall Elimination Provision (WEP) may reduce U.S. benefit

## ESTATE PLANNING FOR SNOWBIRDS

### U.S. Estate Tax Exposure
- U.S. imposes estate tax on worldwide assets of U.S. citizens/residents AND on U.S.-situ assets of non-residents
- **U.S.-situ assets** include: U.S. real property, tangible personal property in U.S., U.S. corporate stocks
- Non-resident exemption: only $60,000 (vs. ~$13M+ for U.S. citizens)
- **BUT** Canada-U.S. Tax Treaty (Article XXIX-B) provides prorated unified credit based on ratio of U.S. assets to worldwide assets

### Treaty Estate Tax Credit Calculation
```
Prorated credit = U.S. unified credit × (U.S.-situ assets / worldwide estate)
```
This effectively provides non-residents a proportional share of the same exemption U.S. citizens receive.

### Additional Credits
- **Marital credit** — Property passing to surviving spouse may qualify for additional credit (effectively doubling the exemption for married couples)
- **Foreign tax credit** — Canada provides credit for U.S. estate tax paid against Canadian deemed disposition tax at death

### Estate Tax Planning Strategies
1. **Equalize U.S. and Canadian asset holdings between spouses** — Each spouse uses their own prorated credit
2. **Joint tenancy with right of survivorship** — Avoids U.S. probate (but doesn't avoid estate tax)
3. **Life insurance** — Use Canadian life insurance to fund U.S. estate tax liability (proceeds not U.S.-situ)
4. **Reduce U.S.-situ assets** — Hold U.S. stocks through Canadian mutual funds or ETFs (Canadian-domiciled fund is not U.S.-situ asset)
5. **Cross-border will** — Separate wills for Canadian and U.S. assets; U.S. will executed under state law where property is located

### Wills and Powers of Attorney
- Canadian will may not be recognized for U.S. property — separate U.S. will recommended
- Powers of attorney may not be recognized across jurisdictions — separate U.S. healthcare directive and financial POA recommended
- Probate is state-specific; some states have simplified procedures for non-residents with real property

## RISK MANAGEMENT FOR SNOWBIRDS

### Health Insurance
- Provincial health insurance may NOT cover medical expenses outside Canada (or coverage is minimal)
- Supplemental travel health insurance is essential
- Pre-existing condition exclusions are common
- Out-of-province coverage rules vary: most provinces require 5–7 months physical presence to maintain eligibility
- U.S. medical costs can be catastrophic without insurance ($50,000+ for a single hospital stay)

### Travel Insurance Key Considerations
- Stability clause: pre-existing conditions must be "stable" for 90–180 days before departure
- Trip cancellation/interruption coverage
- Medical evacuation coverage
- Policy limits and deductibles
- Read exclusions carefully — many claims are denied due to pre-existing condition non-disclosure

## PERMANENT U.S. RESIDENCY CONSIDERATIONS

### Canadian Departure Tax
- Deemed disposition of all assets at fair market value on date of emigration
- Capital gains tax payable on unrealized gains
- Exceptions: Canadian real property, RRSP/RRIF (remain Canadian tax-deferred), pension plans
- Can elect to post security and defer payment

### Key Transition Issues
- TFSA loses tax-exempt status for U.S. purposes immediately
- RRSP/RRIF: protected under Treaty but must file election with IRS annually
- CPP/OAS: continue to be paid; taxed in country of residence
- Provincial health insurance: terminated upon establishing permanent U.S. residence
- U.S. Medicare: eligible at 65 if 10+ years of U.S. work history (40 quarters)
